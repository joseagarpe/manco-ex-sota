a b s t r a c t
The heavy oil is essentially comprised of asphaltene and nitrogen, sulphur, and oxygen-bearing
(NSO) compounds, which records the alteration processes of aerobic biodegradation. In this study,
six biodegraded oils were geochemically examined using Fourier transform ion cyclotron resonance
mass spectrometry (FT-ICR MS) in negative-ion electrospray ionization (ESI(-)) mode to decipher the
NSO heteroatom compounds molecular compositions. The results reveal that the NSO compounds,
especially those strongly resistant to biodegradation, i.e., alcohol/phenols, carboxylic acids with various
alkyl substitution, naphthenic acids with 2 to 5 naphthenic/aromatic rings, nitrogen- and sulphur-
bearing carboxylic acids, and hydroxy acids (O3) and dibasic acids (O4) are enriched in biodegraded
oils. The O1, N1O1, and O1S1 classes appear in trace amounts in severely to extremely biodegraded
oils (PM > 7) but are abundant in moderately biodegraded oils (PM < 7), implying the alcohol group
generated by biodegradation are further oxidized to form a carboxylic acid with the progressively
biodegraded process. Abnormal enrichment of O2 and O4 classes was trapped in the severely–extremely
biodegraded oils, implying the generation of additional monobasic acid/dibasic acids during microbial
aerobic degradation of crude oils. Besides, the relative content of monocyclic naphthenic acid (DBE = 2)
gradually decreased with biodegradation level, while those of the bicyclic-, tricyclic-, and tetracyclic–
naphthenic acid (DBE = 3-5) showed an increasing trend. The newly developed proxies of NSO
compounds (i.e., the ratios of biodegradation products with different numbers of oxygen atoms of the
specified compound, and the O2, O3, O4, and O5 classes with various DBE values and/or various carbon
numbers) agreed well with the existing biodegradation indicators (i.e., C2925-NH/C30H, C2117-NT/C23TT,
C27DS/C27RS, and C28TAS/C21TAS), confirming the availability of ESI(-) FT-ICR MS to distinguish the
biodegraded oils with high resolution.

1. Introduction
Globally, the overwhelming majority of crude oils have exten-
sively undergone a certain extent of secondary alterations (Tissot
and Welte, 1984; Larter et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2016; Huang
and Li, 2017; Hou et al., 2021), i.e., biodegradation, oxidation, and
water washing. The reserves of these heavy fossil fuels, including
extra-heavy oil, oil sands, and bitumen tar maybe even more
than those of traditional crude oils. To decipher the molecular
structural characteristics of heavy oils/asphaltenes, several tech-
nical means have been widely utilized including high-resolution
transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM, Sharma et al., 2002),

The most commonly-used methods for analyzing heavy oils
are traditional GC–MS, GC coupled with tandem mass spectrom-
etry (GC–MS/MS), and GC×GC-TOF MS (Zhang et al., 1988; Mao
et al., 2009; Tran et al., 2010; Rocha et al., 2019; Yang et al.,
2021; Yin et al., 2021). However, as compared to light oils, the
bulk compositions of heavy oils are essentially riched in polar
compounds, i.e., asphaltene, nitrogen, sulphur and oxygen (NSO)
heteroatom moieties, and metals (Ni and V, etc.) exhibit high
molecular weights and strong polarity, which are inhibited from
characterizing comprehensively by the traditional methods.
However, to date, rare compositional insights have been shed
light on the NSO compounds in biodegraded oils. Therefore, mak-
ing full use of the residual asphaltenes and NSO heteroatomic
compounds in biodegraded oils is of profound significance to
investigate the hidden molecular information of heavy oils. How-
ever, limited by acquisition frequency and signal-to-noise ratio
of mass spectrum, when using GC–MS or even GC×GC-TOF MS to
analyze heavy oil, the unresolved complex mixtures bulge has not
been solved yet, and only some tricyclic terpane and adamantane
series with strong bioresistance can be detected.
In contrast, FT-ICR MS, with its characteristics of powerful
mass-to-charge ratio detection and ultra-high mass accuracy, can
bring to light the NSO-compounds, especially naphthenic acids
produced in biodegraded oils, identify molecular structure, and
resolve millions of NSOs that are undetected by GC–MS/FID and
GC×GC-TOF MS/FID. Many geological controversies have recently
been reached utilizing this technology, i.e., thermal maturation
(e.g., Hughey et al., 2004; Poetz et al., 2014; Han et al., 2018;
Rocha et al., 2018; Ventura et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020), oil-
bearing fluid inclusions (Oldenburg et al., 2014; Han et al., 2018,
2020), oil-source rock correlation (Liu et al., 2015; Mahlstedt
et al., 2016; Ji et al., 2018), as well as primary/secondary hydro-
carbon migration (Poetz et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2015; Ziegs et al.,
2018a; Chang et al., 2021).
Therefore, the FT ICR MS can serve as a powerful tool also
could provide a neoteric solution to shed light on NSO het-
eroatom compounds contained in biodegraded oils, enabling the
identification of the differences in NSO heterocyclic compounds
in heavy oils with different biodegradation levels. Molecular
structural information can be deduced by comprehensive anal-

2. Geological backgrounds
2.1. Tectonic settings
The Chepaizi Uplift, which has undergone extensive geophys-
ical investigation, is an inherited palaeo-bulge of the NW Junggar
Basin, China (Fig. 1a). The Chepaizi Uplift is connected to Zaire
Mountains in the northwest, separated by the Aika fault zone and
adjacent to the Sikeshu Sag in the southwest, restricted by the
Hongche fault zone in the east and eastwards to Shawan Sag, and
extends to the northeast, and extending to the northeast to the
Penyijingxi Sag (Fig. 1a, Cao et al., 2010; Xiao et al., 2015; Shi
et al., 2020a,b).
Comprehensive wireline well logging, seismic cross-section
investigation and 1D-basin modeling results demonstrated that
the tectonic evolution of the studied area could be divided into
the following three periods (Fig. 2): (i) Orogenic continuous uplift
stage: Carboniferous-Jurassic; (ii) Slow stratum subsidence stage:
early Cretaceous-Eocene, and (iii) Rapid stratum subsidence stage:
after the Palaeogene (Xia and Jin, 2003; He et al., 2008; Chang
et al., 2019).
2.2. Stratigraphy and lithology
The study area lacks Permian-Triassic and Paleogene inter-
vals, which are subject to continuous erosion and weathering,

3. Experiments and methods
3.1. Samples preparations
79 oil samples or oil sands extracts were collected from the
lower Neogene Shawan Formation (N1s) and upper Carbonifer-
ous Xibeikulasi Formation (C2x) of the studied area, and defined
the extent of biodegradation by parameter-striping method and
refined Manco scale (Xu et al., 2018; Shi et al., 2020a; Chang
et al., 2021). And then, in this study, 6 oil samples were selected
for FT ICR MS testing, which is encoded by their biodegradation
levels at the PM scale (Fig. 1b, c). Of these, 5 samples were at
severe to extreme biodegradation level and 1 oil sample at the
biodegradation level of PM < 7 (Peters and Moldowan, 1993).
Traditional geochemical analyses of these samples were com-
pleted in previous investigations (Xu et al., 2018; Li et al., 2019;
Mao et al., 2020; Shi et al., 2020a; Chang et al., 2021), involving
the physical properties, column chromatography, GC–MS, and
GC–MS/MS.
3.2. FT-ICR MS measurement
6 selected samples were investigated in ESI (-) ionization
mode using a Bruker apex-ultra 9.4 T FT-ICR MS from Bruker
Corporation (Bremen, Germany) and conducted at the State Key
Laboratory of Heavy Oil, China University of Petroleum (Beijing).
For a given CcHhNnOoSs compound, whose double bond equiv-
alent (DBE) value is calculated from DBE = c −h/2 + n/2 + 1
(McLafferty, 1993). DBE value stands for the level of unsaturation,
and composes of both double bonds and aromatic rings (Poetz
et al., 2014).
4. Results and discussion
4.1. Differences in biodegraded levels of heavy oils of eastern Chep-
aizi
The bulk composition, biomarkers, and stable carbon isotopes
proved genetic affinities between the crude oils of eastern Chep-
aizi and Permian oil source of Shawan Sag (Zhang et al., 2012;
Song et al., 2016; Mao et al., 2020; Shi et al., 2020a,b), and
the later fresh charging event virtually was the re-migration of
previously reservoired oils owing to the tectonic tilt movement
(Cao et al., 2010; Xu et al., 2018; Chang et al., 2019, 2021). Hence,
the differences in the geochemical characteristics of different oils
are mainly perturbed by biodegradation.
Oils biodegradation may lead to significant variations in the
physical properties of residual hydrocarbons. The densities of the
crude oils vary from 0.917 g/cm3 to 1.05 g/cm3, the corresponding
viscosity is between 47.51 mPa s and 48,990 mPa s (average:
5089.66 mPa s, Fig. 3), which implies that the biodegraded level
of crude oils in this area varies greatly (Peters and Moldowan,
1993).
Besides, the differential enrichment of 25-norhopane and 17-
nortricyclic terpanes also indicates the difference in the biode-
graded level from heavy (PM7) to extreme (PM9, Seifert et al.,
1980; Volkman et al., 1983; Seifert et al., 1984; Xu et al., 2018;
Li et al., 2019; Shi et al., 2020a,b; Chang et al., 2021). Hence, the
PM scale of oil samples was referenced after Xu et al. (2018) and
Chang et al. (2021) (Table 1). In this study, the gradual increase in
the ratios of C29 25-norhopane to its corresponding biodegraded
parent C30 hopane (C2925-NH/C30H), C22 17-nortricyclic terpane
to its corresponding biodegraded parent C23 tricyclic terpane (C22
17-NT/C23TT), C27 diasterane to its corresponding rearrangement
parent C27 regular sterane (C27DS/C27RS), and long-chain C28 tri-
aromatic steranes (TAS) to their short-chain C21 counterparts
(C28TAS/C21TAS) reveals that from Well P601-p215 to wells P661,
P66, P663, and then to wells P685, P601-x426, the biodegrada-
tion level gradually increases (Rullkotter and Wendisch, 1982;
Ekweozor, 1984; Connan, 1986; Wardroper et al., 1984; Chosson
et al., 1992; Chen et al., 2012). Herein, five samples at heavy to
extreme biodegradation level and another one oil sample (Well
P601-p215) at the biodegradation level of PM < 7 (Fig. 4).

4.2. NSO compounds revealed by ESI(-) FT–ICR MS
Extensive literature indicates that the general aerobic degra-
dation pathways of saturates, aromatics and resins with NSO
heteroatoms mainly include preferentially terminal and/or side-
chain oxidation to form alcohols, then dehydrogenation of alco-
hols to form aldehydes, and finally oxidation to form monoacids
and then hydroxy acids or even dibasic acids (Fig. 5, Denome
et al., 1993; Nhi-Cong et al., 2009; Tomás-Gallardo et al., 2014;
Cheng et al., 2016; Chang et al., 2021. The structures of hopanes
and steranes are relatively stable, and their structural characteris-
tics can be retained during the sequential biodegradation process
to generate corresponding carboxylic acids (Fig. 5g–i, Jiang et al.,
2014; Jaekel et al., 2015; Panasia and Philipp, 2018).
Considering that biodegradation can selectively modify NSO
compounds with different molecular structures, it can be intu-
itively observed that some NSO compounds are selectively re-
moved from the TIC of FT-ICR MS in ESI(-) mode with the pro-
gressively biodegraded levels of crude oils (Fig. 6). Furthermore,
the diversity of species of NSO compounds decreased as biodegra-
dation continued. NSO compounds are now classified accord-
ing to different heteroatom compositions, mainly N1, N2, N1O1,
N1O2, O1-O8, O1S1, O2S1, O3S1 classes, respectively (Fig. 7 and
Table 1). Besides, the distribution of the prominent unimodal
peak is also from C16 to C21. However, the compounds with mul-
tioxygen atoms including O6-O8 are most likely to be pollutants,
hence, we do not discuss their changes with increasing level of
biodegradation.
The distribution of heteroatom-containing compounds in oils
with different biodegraded level shows that when the biode-
graded level reaches the severe–extreme stage, O1, N1, N1O1, and
O1S1 classes are only in trace amounts, while O2 classes gradually
become the sole dominant compound, with considerable amount
of O3-O5 classes (Fig. 8). Relative abundance of O5-O8 classes
remains at trace levels in severely to extremely biodegraded oils
despite being suppressed by abundant O2 and O4 classes (Fig. 8b).
The N1 classes may consist of a pyrrole ring and various aro-
matic and/or naphthenic rings. The N1 compounds in this study
are dominant by carbazoles (DBE = 9), benzocarbazoles (DBE
= 12), and dibenzocarbazoles (DBE = 15), respectively (Hughey
et al., 2007, Zhang et al., 2020; Fig. 8c). The trace amounts of
N1 classes are present in severely to extremely biodegraded oils
and are abundant in oils with PM < 7, implying the N1 classes
exhibit a decreasing tendency with sequential biodegradation
from light to extreme, while the content of N1O1 classes feathered
the trending of increases first and then decreases. The N1O1 class
species are the most abundant NxOz compounds, and fluorenono-
carbazoles were indicated as possible structures for N1O1 classes
DBE 15, 18 and 21 by Poetz et al. (2014). The corresponding

4.2.1. Phenols/alcohols
Among the O1 compounds, DBE = 1 mainly corresponds to
alcohol compounds, DBE = 4 corresponds to alkylphenols, and
DBE = 5 and 6 mainly correspond to alkylphenols with indanol
rings. And DBE = 8 may be especially alkylnaphthols compounds
(Fig.S1, Shi et al., 2010a,b; Oldenburg et al., 2014). N1O1 and
O1S1 classes were identified as nitrogen/sulphur-containing phe-
nols/alcohols, which are considered intermediates formed by
biodegradation of N1/S1 classes (Kim et al., 2005).
As illustrated in Figs. 7 and 8, O1, N1O1, and O1S1 are in
trace or undetectable in severely to extremely biodegraded oils
but abundant in oils with PM < 7 (Well P601-p215), implying
that alcohol groups are considered as intermediate products of
oil biodegradation. These alkylphenols favor being completely
biodegraded and consumed under conditions that are vulnerable
to more intense biodegradation Watson et al., 2002. The relatively
wide distribution of O1 and range of DBE values in Well P601-
x426 compared to the less weathered samples Well P663 and
Well P685 maybe suggest that the sample was contaminated with
a mixture of severely biodegraded and amounts of light-degraded
oils.

4.2.2. Carboxylic acids
The cross-plots of DBE vs carbon number indicate that, in
the moderately biodegraded oils, the O2 classes are predomi-
nate by DBE1, with the further deepening of the biodegraded
level (PM > 7), fatty acids and isoprenoid acids (DBE = 1–2)
have further undergone decarboxylation and disappeared, except
for C16H32O2 and C18H36O2 (interpreted as palmitic acid and
stearic acid, respectively), which are most likely contamination of
polypropylene tubing from ESI sources (Pan et al., 2013). While
when the biodegraded level reaches severe, the O2 compounds
with bicyclic acids (DBE = 3) gradually dominate, and then when
the biodegraded level reaches extreme, the O2 compounds with
tricyclic, tetracyclic acids (DBE = 4–5) dominate progressively.
The dominance of bicyclic terpanic acids and tricyclic terpanic
acids that residual in the extremely biodegraded oils indicate that
they, especially tricyclic terpanic acids, are strongly counteract
to microbial aerobic degradation or may be caused by cracking
of the compounds with polyrings (Fig. 10a–b and Table 2, Cheng
et al., 2016; Wang and Zhang, 2022). Besides, the carbon number
also exhibited regular changes, that is, the high carbon number
(C18-C23) predominates the severely–extremely biodegraded oil
(Fig. 10c).
Previous studies show that O2 compounds in ESI(-) mode
mainly are acidic compounds containing phenolic hydroxyl or
carboxyl groups (R-COOH). Herein, DBE = 1 were identified as
linear and isoprenoid acids (Kim et al., 2005; Vaz et al., 2013; Lalli
et al., 2015). The DBE 5 compounds with 27, 28, and 29 carbon
atoms represented by 4-ring steranoic acid, that is, cholestanic,
ergostanic and stigmastanic acids, respectively (Rodrigues et al.,
2000; Poetz et al., 2014; Lalli et al., 2015; Rocha et al., 2018), are
relatively deficient in the unaltered sample while progressively
increase with ongoing biodegradation. And O2 class compounds
with DBE values of 6 and carbon numbers from 28 to 36, repre-
senting hopanoic acids (Shi et al., 2010a,b; Liao et al., 2012; Poetz
et al., 2014; Rocha et al., 2018).
The presence of hopanoic acids has been reported for geo-
logical samples having different origins, maturity and biodegra-
dation levels (Behar and Albrecht, 1984; Jaffé et al., 1988a,b).
The abundance of C31,32Hn−10O2 hopanoic acids (DBE = 6) ini-
tially increases (PM1–2) then decreases (PM3+). In the severely
biodegraded oil, the hopanoic acids are almost totally depleted,
while terpanic C21, C24 and C26 acids remain prominent, confirm-
ing their resistance to biodegradation. Oxidation of the choles-
terol C27 methyl group to a primary alcohol or carboxylic acid
group by vertebrates and conversion of cholesterol to trihydrox-
ycoprostacic acid and cholic acid were reported. The relative
abundance of steranes and corresponding steranoic acids confirm
this conclusion. As seen in Fig. 10c, the C27-C29 steranoic acid
and C29-C31 hopanic acid dominate in the severe to extreme
biodegradation stage.
However, interestingly, hopanes were substantially altered
in the P66 and P663 oils, while almost completely eliminated
in the P685 and P601-x426 oils, but exhibit undetectable or
trace amounts of hopanoic acids in those oils (Figs. 4a, and
10a, b). Analogously, undetectable or trace amounts of 4-ring
steranoic acids were observed with-substantial consumption of
steranes in the P685 and P601-x426 oils (Figs. 4c, and 10a,
b). All of those may imply another pathway for biodegrada-
tion reactions, rather than terminal or side-chain oxidation of
hopanes and steranes, which do not produce the corresponding
acidic compounds. As mentioned above, 25-nor-hopanes and 17-
nor-tricyclic terpanes were recognized in moderately–heavily
and heavily–extremely biodegraded oils, respectively, and were
enriched in the heavily and extremely biodegraded oils, respec-
tively, by demethylation of hopanes (Lamorde et al., 2015; Killops
et al., 2019). 25-norhopanoic acids (C28-C31) and hopanoic acids
(C29-C34) were highly enriched in moderately biodegraded oils,
while decreased in heavily biodegraded oils, but absent or in trace
amounts in lightly biodegraded oils (Fig. 5). Correspondingly,
the 17-nor-tricyclic terpanic acids (C19-C25) and tricyclic terpanic
acids (C19-C26) were highly enriched in heavily biodegraded oils,
while decreased in extremely biodegraded oils, but absent or in
trace amounts lightly to moderately biodegraded oils. Following
the biodegradation scales proposed by Peters and Moldowan
(1993) and Killops et al. (2019), the regular steranes and hopanes
of oils were substantially consumed and completely eliminated at
PM6 and PM8, respectively; while the tricyclic terpanes were not
substantially consumed until PM8. Hence, systematic variations
in the relative abundance of these new biomarkers with the ex-
tent of biodegradation implies that these compounds can served
as new proxies for biodegradation.

4.2.3. Acidic compounds with multioxygen atoms
O3 classes in ESI(-) mode may be hydroxycarboxylic acid com-
pounds, and O4 classes may be binary acids or dihydric alcohol
acids, while the O5 classes in negative-ion ESI mode may be
aldehyde diacids (Ziegs et al., 2018b; Wang and Zhu, 2019). Fig.S3
shows the bubble charts of DBE vs carbon number for the O3
classes. The O3 classes generally distribute over carbon numbers
of 10–34 with a DBE range of 1–17 in severely–extremely biode-
graded oils, while absent in moderately biodegraded oil (Well
P601-p215). The components with carbon numbers of 15–25
and DBEs of 2–10 are exhibit high relative content, especially in
the severely biodegraded oils but relative descends in extremely
biodegraded oils, which reveals the presence of O3 classes that
span a wide range of carbon number and DBE value (aromaticity).
The O4 classes in severely–extremely biodegraded oils have a
carbon number of 14, and those with DBE values of 6–7 are dom-
inant constituents, interpreted as the diacids of benzocyclohex-
ane and hexahydronaphthalene (Fig.S3). This is the second most
abundant compound class in severely–extremely biodegraded oils
(Fig. 7), and even more dominated by higher DBE classes, mean-
ing that this compound class contains more aromatic moieties
than O2 and O3 compounds. The content of O5 compounds with
chemical formulas [C14H17O5]−, [C15H19O5]−, −and [C17H25O5] in
ESI(-) mode are dominant in both moderately biodegraded and
severely–extremely biodegraded oils. According to the DBE cal-
culation rules, those O5 compounds mentioned above are inter-
preted as aldehyde diacids of nonahydronaphthalene and decalin
(Fig.S4, Niles et al., 2019).
4.2.4. New proxies to characterize NSO compounds
Dyreborg et al. (1997) analyzed the biodegradation of NSO
compounds under different redox conditions. Watson et al. (2002)
found in laboratory biodegradation studies that once formed,
lower molecular weight acids (C10-C20) were rapidly biodegraded,
while higher molecular weight acids (>C20) were recalcitrant.
These recalcitrant acids could potentially be employed as molecu-
lar monitors of the biodegradation process in microbial alteration.
Previous studies also proposed that the resistance of NSO het-
eroatomic compounds to biodegradation is similar to those of
hydrocarbons, and would undergo a complex selective biodegra-
dation process, and proposed that the ratio of fatty acid to naph-
thenic acid can be utilized to quantify the extent of biodegra-
dation (Kim et al., 2005; Scott et al., 2005; Han et al., 2008).
Lima et al. (2010) demonstrated that the hopanoic acids with the
C32 ββR biological configuration and a suite of 3-propylsteranoic
acids with the carboxylic function in the C-17 side chain can
serve as new proxies for biodegradation. 1–2 ring naphthenic
acids (DBE = 2–3) are more resistant to biodegradation than 4–5

absent.
